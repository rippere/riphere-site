Riphere LLC: AI for files that can't leave the building

The call The partner signs off on a cited answer. The files never left the office.

I'm building a box that runs AI inside the office, for law firms and other organizations whose records can't go to someone else's server. Every answer cites the page it came from.

5 min read

Why I built it

Because the market needed it. A lot of the most valuable records sit behind walls: case files, patient charts, controlled defense documents. The people who hold them want AI on them, and they can't send them to someone else's server without answering for who can see them.

The rules are catching up. Starting in 2027, California lets a lawyer put confidential client information into an AI system only if access is limited to the attorney and people bound to keep it confidential (SB 574). Defense contractors that keep controlled unclassified information in a cloud service already have to hold that provider to the federal FedRAMP Moderate baseline (DFARS 252.204-7012). Meanwhile most small law firms already use AI, 75% in Clio's survey, and only 9% have a written, enforced AI policy, per 8am (both reported by the NC Bar).

I started out quoting custom builds one firm at a time. Every dollar of that cost my own hours, so I'm building one box, with swappable workflow packs, that any of these offices can run.

THE PROBLEM

Who it's for

First, small law firms whose cases run on medical records: workers' comp, personal injury, disability. The files are long. In California workers' comp, the average medical-legal evaluation that billed for extra record review covered about 1,100 pages (CHSWC 2025). After that, any office holding records it can't hand to a vendor.

A box in the office costs more up front than a hosted tool, and it buys control. When the system is yours, you know who is accessing it and who can get into it, and you can change it yourself. A firm has to answer for its own work, and that's easier when the system reading its files is its own. The compute belongs to the firm too, so a question costs electricity instead of a per-page fee.

Hosted terms can also change under you. In February 2026 a federal court held that documents a client made with a public chatbot weren't privileged, in part because the tool's privacy policy allowed disclosure to third parties (Heppner).

MY WORK

What it does

Today the demo does the core job. You drop PDFs in, and it reads them page by page, with OCR for scanned pages. You ask a question in plain English. Every claim in the answer cites the document and page it came from, and clicking a citation opens that page. If the answer isn't in the documents, it says so and cites nothing.

It runs on an AI workstation I own, against an open-weight model, with the documents stored on the box. The demo uses synthetic records only: a fictional workers' comp case file built around a generated patient.

The citations are there because a person still signs off. In 2026 California's workers' comp appeals board sanctioned a lawyer $1,000 over fabricated AI quotations, and said a filing that misstates facts or law is sanctionable "whether or not it was prepared with the assistance of AI" (Lopez Mendoza). A cited page gives the attorney something to check before signing.

How I build it

I write Riphere's software, like the rest of my work, with AI coding agents. The design, the decisions and the liability are mine.

The agents run under rules. They work on their own branches. Where a repo has real tests running in CI, a change can merge once they pass; everywhere else it waits for my review. A separate agent checks NovaCRM's four services every 15 minutes, fixes known failures up to three times, then hands the problem to me and stops.

Those rules came from getting it wrong. Over the summer I ran a version that found its own work and shipped it unattended. 31 of its 36 pull requests merged, and then it stopped producing anything useful. I named the option to rip it out on August 31 and let it run eleven more weeks before I switched it off on November 17. I rebuilt it to merge only on passing CI and to shut itself off when its output drops. The part that finds its own work now runs in shadow mode: it logs what it would do, and turning it on is my call.

What's next

  3. Corrections the box remembers, so a mistake fixed once stays fixed. Nothing is retrained and nothing leaves the box.

  5. A report that maps all of it to the controls an auditor asks about.

Then a first firm running it on its own records.
