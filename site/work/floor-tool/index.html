Floor tool: the rep answers on the spot

A floor-planning tool that turns a measured room into a graded game count, built as an employee of an arcade-equipment distributor.

5 min read

Why I built it

I was hired as an intern to support the CAD design team. I'd been building with AI for a while, and once I'd shown I could take a problem several people were working around and give it a direction, I was brought on full time. The job was to find the bottlenecks and streamline long-standing processes, making parts of the workflow role-agnostic, so that anyone who's asked to do the work is able to.

My first plan was big. A 3D twin of each customer's game room, built on a physics simulator and 3D modeling software, showing live revenue and preventative-maintenance timers. Under it, a simulated-annealing optimizer to arrange the floor for the best egress paths and revenue flow. My eyes were bigger than my stomach. The live data was going to come from processes and standards I'd assumed were in place, and in a long-established business much of that knowledge lived in drawings, files and people's experience, not in feeds a twin could read. It might have been a great solve for a company. It wasn't the right solve for this one, not yet.

What I learned: solve for what's needed, not for what I imagine is needed. And what was needed was simple. Every deal starts with one question: how many games fit in this room?

Who it's for

Two groups, on either end of one question.

The sales rep, talking with a customer about a room. The customer asks how many games fit, and often doesn't have every detail of the room to hand. The answer used to be "I'll get back to you." The count went into the loop with the person in charge of the architectural plans, and the rep kept checking in until it came back. It was a major pain point.

The CAD team, on the other end of every one of those requests. Each rough count landed on them on top of the drawing work only they can do.

Now, with the room's measurements, or a drawing of the room with its scale, the rep makes the count and gets things going. It gives them power in the conversation that isn't only about getting people to buy. The CAD team has a lot less on its shoulders, sales doesn't wait on them, and the queue is much shorter, if there is one at all. People are excited about this tool, and they like using it.

What it does

The count used to be made by hand in CAD: a usable area drawn over the plan, divided by a fixed rule of thumb. The floor tool makes the same count in a browser, from whatever the room arrives as: a CAD file, a PDF, a napkin sketch or an email.

  • Three ways in. Type a rectangle, trace an irregular room, or upload a plan or photo and set its scale. Curved walls work.
  • Doors measured inside. A door is taken as the clear opening inside the frame, not the frame itself, because a game has to fit through what's actually open.
  • A count with its reasons. The tool works out the usable area and the game count, and grades the count high, medium or low. The reasons for the grade are written out in plain words, so the rep can say why.
  • Data stays on site. Every language model runs locally, behind a guard that blocks outbound connections while proprietary data is being processed.

The gameroom twin

The 3D twin from my first plan came back, narrower. After an install, the twin keeps a record of the customer's floor, machine by machine, and runs a preventative-maintenance program on top of it, with a phone view a technician can work from. Maintenance comes first. The revenue side waits until the data it needs is in place.

How I got here

I spent a couple of months in the drawing archives finding the primitives and files the process ran on, so the knowledge transfer could even begin.

Then the call I got wrong first. I wanted the tool to solve for all of the design process, and so did the engineer I worked alongside. When it was clear he wanted to stick with his own CAD tools, I chased parity between the two, to win him over. What tipped me off was that no salesperson is going to have in-depth knowledge of how electrical works. People don't want to be replaced by the machine, and he wanted his expertise to be made useful. So I scoped the tool for sales and met the engineer in his own tool instead of replacing it. The tool hands him a DXF file, which his CAD opens and saves as a DWG with nothing lost.

Another call on the way:

  • The optimizer, parked. I started with a constraint solver, had the design adversarially reviewed on purpose, and the review showed it wouldn't scale. So I moved to a technique from warehouse slotting: simulated annealing over the order games get placed in. It's built and tested, and it stays switched off until there are real installed floors to optimize against.

Results

  • Used by the sales and CAD teams.
  • Runs on the company's own cloud, behind company sign-in, with each release pinned to an exact build.
  • The gameroom twin is piloting.

What's next

Next is automating the count end to end, and taking the gameroom twin past its pilot.

Some of the company's apps have since been built by describing them to an AI app builder, including a version of the twin. I run the path those apps take to production. The rule I set: the builder is a sandbox, and a change reaches the company's own repo and cloud only as a pull request I approve. A review harness I built compares each export with the repo, matches each change to the request behind it, checks it against risk rules and never approves anything itself.

Nobody is changing those apps right now, so the work is designing the path, not containing damage. Next, written house rules go into the existing apps before any more building. I'm also tightening what coworkers who don't code can send in, and making sure every request ends approved or denied, with the reason sent back to whoever asked. The twin goes first; a few of the company's other apps will eventually need the same path, and we're still identifying which ones together. Each piece of the harness starts with a check I've reviewed before it's built, and anything a check can't judge, I sign off by hand.
