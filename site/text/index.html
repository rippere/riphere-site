FL 36 · employer

Floor tool

A floor-planning tool that turns a measured room into a graded game count, built as an employee of an arcade-equipment distributor.

I built it as an employee · an arcade-equipment distributor

The call The rep answers on the spot, not “I’ll get back to you.”

The problem

For an arcade-game sales rep

Two groups, on either end of one question.

The sales rep, talking with a customer about a room. The customer asks how many games fit, and often doesn't have every detail of the room to hand. The answer used to be "I'll get back to you." The count went into the loop with the person in charge of the architectural plans, and the rep kept checking in until it came back. It was a major pain point.

The CAD team, on the other end of every one of those requests. Each rough count landed on them on top of the drawing work only they can do.

Now, with the room's measurements, or a drawing of the room with its scale, the rep makes the count and gets things going. It gives them power in the conversation that isn't only about getting people to buy. The CAD team has a lot less on its shoulders, sales doesn't wait on them, and the queue is much shorter, if there is one at all. People are excited about this tool, and they like using it.

My work

It turns a measured room into a game count, graded, with its reasons.

The count used to be made by hand in CAD: a usable area drawn over the plan, divided by a fixed rule of thumb. The floor tool makes the same count in a browser, from whatever the room arrives as: a CAD file, a PDF, a napkin sketch or an email.

  • Three ways in. Type a rectangle, trace an irregular room, or upload a plan or photo and set its scale. Curved walls work.
  • Doors measured inside. A door is taken as the clear opening inside the frame, not the frame itself, because a game has to fit through what's actually open.
  • A count with its reasons. The tool works out the usable area and the game count, and grades the count high, medium or low. The reasons for the grade are written out in plain words, so the rep can say why.
  • Data stays on site. Every language model runs locally, behind a guard that blocks outbound connections while proprietary data is being processed.

Receipt

  • Used by the sales and CAD teams. verified
  • Runs on the company's own cloud, behind company sign-in, with each release pinned to an exact build.
  • The gameroom twin is piloting.

FL 26 · own build

NovaCRM

An AI-native CRM that turns scattered email and Slack into a prioritized pipeline, and never sends anything without a person's yes.

I built it

The call The follow-up is already written. You just say yes.

The problem

For a founder who sells

A founder who sells. The kind of person who still owes a reply from 16 days ago, not because they don't care, but because the inbox, the Slack threads and the deal notes all live in different places and none of them tap you on the shoulder.

My work

It drafts your follow-ups, then waits for your yes.

Agents take the scattered email and Slack and turn it into an organized, prioritized pipeline, with a person in the loop on anything that matters.

  • Replies. Open a message and it drafts the reply: subject, body and tone, from the message and the contact.
  • Quiet deals. Every deal carries a health score that drops when it sits too long in a stage or the other side goes silent. Once a day, the deals that have gone quiet get a drafted follow-up, posted to Slack with two buttons: Approve & Send, or Dismiss.
  • The rest of the pipeline. Lead scoring, call transcription and search over contacts by meaning, not just by name.

The follow-up is already written. You just say yes.

Receipt

  • In use by a second department. verified
  • Live. It runs at app.riphere.com, with the API, background workers and database deployed alongside it.
  • From the code: the drafted follow-up sends only when a person approves it; the quiet-deal check runs once a day, drafts for at most 5 deals, and won't ask about the same deal again for 7 days.

FL 18 · own research

PrePost

A second-by-second read of creator video, built on a brain-response model, and tested in public before I trust it.

I built it · used for a WSU class project

The call The campaign manager cuts the weak ad before the budget goes.

The problem

For campaign managers

People who approve or send back 20 or more creator videos a month: agency campaign managers, brand creator-program leads, the strategists picking which creator ads get budget.

Today they watch the draft and go on feel. The note back to the creator is "it feels slow," which the creator can't act on, and the bar moves depending on who reviewed it. Guessing wrong costs money, impressions and impact.

My work

A model reads an ad second by second. It runs on Meta’s TRIBE v2.

PrePost turns that feeling into a timestamp. Instead of "it feels slow," the note reads "the predicted response sags at 0:04, tighten the open." Every draft on a campaign gets the same read, so approvals rest on one standard instead of whoever was reviewing that day.

Under the hood it runs Meta's TRIBE v2 (d'Ascoli et al., 2026), a model trained on brain scans of people watching and listening to media. Give it a new video and it predicts how an average viewer's brain would respond, second by second. PrePost maps that onto seven named brain areas and writes the result up in plain English first, with the science underneath.

Why a model and not a scanner: it isn't only that I can't pay for scanning. The rigor, setting and support a feasible, defensible scan study needs is far beyond what a bachelor's degree holder studying this on his own can reach. That's not to say I haven't tried.

Receipt

  • 64 published ad clips scored on hardware I run verified

The pilot: I set a bar and missed it

On May 10, 2026 I wrote the pass bar into the repo. The main bar: the composite score had to correlate with engagement at r above 0.4. The first result came back on May 13: r = 0.25 (p = 0.23, n = 24, engagement measured as likes per view). That's short of the bar, and it could have been chance.

The pilot couldn't have answered the question anyway: its 24 videos came from 3 creators, so the real sample was closer to 3 than 24. That's why the next test is designed the way it is (below), and why PrePost is offered as a diagnostic, not a forecast.

The 64-clip run: early patterns, not findings

I then scored 64 published short-form video ads from YouTube Shorts on hardware I run myself: 64 of 64 scored, none failed, and a re-score from scratch came back bit-identical. What came out of it:

  • One yardstick works across categories. Beauty, food, home and tech ads didn't need separate scales; category explained little of the spread.
  • Creator-style beat polished. UGC-format ads scored above polished spots on the composite (p = 0.004).
  • Human-made beat AI-made. Human-made ads drew a 15% stronger predicted whole-brain response than AI-made ones (p = 0.003, 21 human vs 40 AI).

The caveats are real: a small sample shaped by YouTube search, human/AI labels that are judgements rather than disclosures, a prediction for a typical brain rather than real viewers, and no performance data yet. The human/AI gap is a group difference, not a detector; individual clips overlap a lot. Linking these scores to how ads actually perform is the next step.

FL 08 · the company

Riphere LLC

I'm building a box that runs AI inside the office, for law firms and other organizations whose records can't go to someone else's server. Every answer cites the page it came from.

Riphere LLC · I founded it

The call The partner signs off on a cited answer. The files never left the office.

The problem

For a small law or clinical practice

First, small law firms whose cases run on medical records: workers' comp, personal injury, disability. The files are long. In California workers' comp, the average medical-legal evaluation that billed for extra record review covered about 1,100 pages (CHSWC 2025). After that, any office holding records it can't hand to a vendor.

A box in the office costs more up front than a hosted tool, and it buys control. When the system is yours, you know who is accessing it and who can get into it, and you can change it yourself. A firm has to answer for its own work, and that's easier when the system reading its files is its own. The compute belongs to the firm too, so a question costs electricity instead of a per-page fee.

Hosted terms can also change under you. In February 2026 a federal court held that documents a client made with a public chatbot weren't privileged, in part because the tool's privacy policy allowed disclosure to third parties (Heppner).

My work

Private AI that stays on your premises.

Today the demo does the core job. You drop PDFs in, and it reads them page by page, with OCR for scanned pages. You ask a question in plain English. Every claim in the answer cites the document and page it came from, and clicking a citation opens that page. If the answer isn't in the documents, it says so and cites nothing.

It runs on an AI workstation I own, against an open-weight model, with the documents stored on the box. The demo uses synthetic records only: a fictional workers' comp case file built around a generated patient.

The citations are there because a person still signs off. In 2026 California's workers' comp appeals board sanctioned a lawyer $1,000 over fabricated AI quotations, and said a filing that misstates facts or law is sanctionable "whether or not it was prepared with the assistance of AI" (Lopez Mendoza). A cited page gives the attorney something to check before signing.
